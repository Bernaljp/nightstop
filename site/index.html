the problem

Nobody answers the only question the crew member has.

Every month a PDF arrives with thirty days of duty on it: early starts, night sectors, standby windows, layovers in cities several hours from your own body clock. Operator systems check whether the roster is legal, which is the company's problem. The crew member's problem is when do I go to bed on the 14th, and the answer depends on where their body clock has actually got to, how much sleep debt has built up, and how much of the rest period the commute eats at both ends.

So they work it out by hand, or they paste the roster into a chatbot. I measured the second one.

reads the roster at 99.3%
trustworthy plans 0/8
silently wrong 8/8

A chatbot reads an unfamiliar roster well and still produces a plan you cannot rely on, in every case. Most of those read the month perfectly and withheld every real collision — nobody had shown it your operator's manual. It also cited 69 rules that do not exist.

The fatigue science is not the scarce thing. What is scarce is a system that reads your roster exactly right, knows your rules, and tells you which of them your month breaks.

the design

The model reads. The arithmetic does not.

Every airline lays a roster out differently — Spanish column headers, times in UTC only, report time you have to derive from an offset table, continuation rows that drop the date, a duty crossing midnight with nothing marking the day change. Reading that is the one job here that genuinely needs a language model, and no per-airline parser scales past the first airline.

Everything after it is arithmetic, and arithmetic from a model is arithmetic you have to check anyway.

It always produces a schedule

Where the roster forces a collision, it is stated with something you could actually do about it. 14 CFR 117.25(f) puts the judgement about sleep opportunity on the crew member by name — a tool that quietly decided would be taking a decision the regulation says is theirs.

Your hours, not a default

You tell it when you normally sleep — 21:30 or midnight, they differ by hours — and it holds that rhythm where the roster allows, says how far it had to move you where it does not, and puts recovery sleep after a duty that took your usual window off you.

It says what it worked out

A value printed on the page and a value inferred from it are not the same kind of fact, and only one can be checked against the document. Derived values are labelled and shown for confirmation.

It ends up in your calendar

The plan comes out as an .ics — every block with its reasoning attached, duties marked free so it never blocks your own diary. No model-facing tool can write to a calendar; importing the file is you deciding, on your device.

Read across it. Giving it the rules is most of the value and nowhere near enough. Tools fix the reading completely, and it stays fixed. And then the model finds every single real collision — 100% recall — and still fails seven of eight cases, because it invents forty more, and costs more doing it.

The last column changes one thing: the rule check moves out of the model and into a function.

The model was never the bottleneck on finding collisions. It was the bottleneck on not inventing them. A deterministic checker cannot invent a rule — the failure mode is removed by construction rather than discouraged by prompting. So the biggest single improvement here came from taking work away from the model.

Held out

Four more rosters from a seed never used before, generated after the configuration was re-frozen and run once — every hard feature at once, a roster printing no totals so the reader's self-check is simply absent, Spanish labels on a daylight-saving month, single-character codes where a day off is a bare hyphen.

chatbot, trustworthy 0/4
chatbot, silently wrong 4/4
nightstop, trustworthy 4/4
nightstop, silently wrong 0/4
Why a second set. There was an earlier one, frozen sooner. The engine then changed seven times — naps, night coverage, two rules about shifting bedtime, the hours each crew member actually keeps, time to get through the door, and how much sleep a day awake buys — and every change made that set less held out, because it had already been seen. Amending a pre-registration once is honest; doing it seven times and still calling the number held out is not. Both sets are reported and never pooled. The reader is the half that has never been tuned, and npm run verify:freeze checks that claim rather than asserting it, reporting the planner's drift openly.

what no number caught

Ten of fourteen stages moved neither headline number.

All ten were real defects. A planner that never once suggested a nap. One that left every day off unplanned. One that gave every crew member the same bedtime whatever they had told it. One that stacked three sleeps into thirteen hours with a five-minute gap. One that had people asleep the minute they walked through the door. One that asked for twenty-four hours of sleep in a forty-six hour layover, and printed “the roster leaves no room to keep your normal hours” underneath it — on a window with two clear days in it.

And a calendar that drew each block in its own station’s timezone, so a Madrid–New York sector ended at 21:10 and the sleep after it began at 19:56. The picture showed a crew member asleep an hour and a half before they landed. The plan was right. The drawing of it was not, and a drawing is the whole product.

Then I fixed the geometry, left the captions in station time, and shipped a block sitting at 00:41 on the axis labelled “ORD 17:41”. The same reader found the same bug a second time. A partial fix to a display bug is indistinguishable from a complete one if you only ever check it from a terminal. One clock now sets position, label and list together, with a picker that moves the whole view at once — and the reasons carry durations only, never a clock reading that could contradict it.

And the fix for one of those defects caused the next. Capping a rest period at a single extra nap stopped three sleeps stacking up, and left a crew member landing off a red-eye at 14:20 with nothing offered until midnight. Nine hours awake, no nap. The two naps have their own slots now — recovery before the first night, pre-duty after the last — and recovery sleep across sixteen rosters went from none to sixty-six.

Then I wrote the test suite, turned nine sessions of hindsight into thirteen invariants, and the first run failed one. Finish standby at 13:00, report at 23:00 for an all-night sector, and the eight hours between held no block at all — no sleep, no nap, no conflict, no warning. The answer to “no number could see it” was not a better number. It was a different kind of check. An assertion that every window long enough to sleep in gets sleep in it found in three seconds what twelve cases, four repeats and two held-out sets had walked straight past.

defects a score caught 0 of 10
found by reading the output 9
found by an invariant 1
Twelve rosters, a pre-registered metric, an independent grader, four repeats and two held-out sets — and every one of these sailed straight through. The grader asks whether a plan breaks a rule. It never asked whether a person could follow the plan, or whether the picture of it told the truth.

what i removed

The experiment that tied, and was cut anyway.

When the reader flags a value as uncertain, hand it back to the model on its own and ask it to resolve it. Forty lines, targets exactly the values most likely to be wrong. I predicted it would beat the primary metric while making the system more dangerous — a clean story about a metric rewarding the wrong thing.

It did something worse. It tied: same trustworthy count, same silently-wrong count, identical field accuracy and recall.

values a crew member can check, shipped 33
with the repair pass 0
cost +38%

Thirty-three derived values, each with its reasoning attached, become zero. The pass guessed right every time on this corpus, which is not a reason to keep it — the behaviour is identical whether the guess is right or wrong, so the only thing between a pilot and a confidently wrong report time is that the model happened to be correct.

hot take

A good metric is necessary, and it is not sufficient.

The two worst bugs in this project were in the measuring apparatus, not the thing being measured, and both would have flattered the result.

The grader compared timestamps as strings, so 08:00:00.000Z and 08:00:00Z counted as a misread — it reported 78% accuracy on a roster read perfectly. My answer key was wrong about days off, recording every one as being at base; the reader disagreed with me on six fields and was right about all six.

Then the fix for the last failing case turned out not to be a rule or a check, but asking the model to say which values it had worked out rather than read. Being made to show its work changed the work.

And the experiment I removed tied on every number I report while taking the count of checkable values from 33 to zero. My metric could not see the difference — which is worse than being fooled by it, because being fooled at least leaves a trace.

Build the scoreboard before the agent. Then distrust the scoreboard too, and go and look at what the person actually ends up holding.

Advisory only. Not a flight time limitations tool; it makes no ruling on whether any duty is legal. Every airline, roster and crew reference in the project is invented — no real person's schedule appears anywhere in it.
