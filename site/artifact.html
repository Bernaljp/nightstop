the problem

Nobody answers the only question the crew member has.

Every month a PDF arrives with thirty days of duty on it: early starts, night sectors, standby windows, layovers in cities several hours from your own body clock. Operator systems check whether the roster is legal, which is the company's problem. The crew member's problem is when do I go to bed on the 14th, and the answer depends on where their body clock has actually got to, how much sleep debt has built up, and how much of the rest period the commute eats at both ends.

So they work it out by hand, or they paste the roster into a chatbot. I measured the second one.

reads the roster at 99.3%
trustworthy plans 0/8
silently wrong 8/8

A chatbot reads an unfamiliar roster well and still produces a plan you cannot rely on, in every case. Most of those read the month perfectly and withheld every real collision — nobody had shown it your operator's manual. It also cited 69 rules that do not exist.

The fatigue science is not the scarce thing. What is scarce is a system that reads your roster exactly right, knows your rules, and tells you which of them your month breaks.

the design

The model reads. The arithmetic does not.

Every airline lays a roster out differently — Spanish column headers, times in UTC only, report time you have to derive from an offset table, continuation rows that drop the date, a duty crossing midnight with nothing marking the day change. Reading that is the one job here that genuinely needs a language model, and no per-airline parser scales past the first airline.

Everything after it is arithmetic, and arithmetic from a model is arithmetic you have to check anyway.

roster PDF ─► READER (model) tools, not trust: to_utc the hours that repeat, and the ones that never happen reconcile_totals the document grading its own transcription │ ▼ ENGINE (deterministic) places sleep, finds every rule collision │ ▼ BRIEFING what needs a decision, then the month, then the detail

It always produces a schedule

Where the roster forces a collision, it is stated with something you could actually do about it. 14 CFR 117.25(f) puts the judgement about sleep opportunity on the crew member by name — a tool that quietly decided would be taking a decision the regulation says is theirs.

Three kinds of rule, never conflated

A hard limit from your regulator, a recommendation from fatigue science or company policy, and your own line that only you can move. Point it at your operator's manual and it pulls out the four paragraphs that touch sleep.

It says what it worked out

A value printed on the page and a value inferred from it are not the same kind of fact, and only one can be checked against the document. Derived values are labelled and shown for confirmation.

It ends up in your calendar

The plan comes out as an .ics — every block with its reasoning attached, duties marked free so it never blocks your own diary. No model-facing tool can write to a calendar; importing the file is you deciding, on your device.

A plan counts as trustworthy only if every duty-bearing field matches ground truth exactly, every collision the roster forces is surfaced, and nothing is raised that is not real. Silently wrong is reported at equal size because it is the only outcome that can hurt someone — a binary alone hides the difference between wrong and flagged.

Held out

Four more rosters, generated from a separate seed after every prompt was frozen and not run against until the end — every hard feature at once, a roster printing no totals so the reader's self-check is simply absent, Spanish labels on a daylight-saving month, single-character codes where a day off is a bare hyphen.

chatbot, trustworthy 0/4
chatbot, silently wrong 3/4
nightstop, trustworthy 4/4
nightstop, silently wrong 0/4
How that number was reached. The first held-out run scored 3/4, and the failure was a bug in my corpus — a UTC-only roster dated its rows by base local day, so the date column and the time column disagreed with nothing to say which to believe. The reader transcribed it as printed and flagged the overlap as probably a source error, which was the better of the two readings. No prompt, tool or rule was changed at any point, and npm run verify:freeze proves it rather than asserting it.

what i removed

The experiment that tied, and was cut anyway.

When the reader flags a value as uncertain, hand it back to the model on its own and ask it to resolve it. Forty lines, targets exactly the values most likely to be wrong. I predicted it would beat the primary metric while making the system more dangerous — a clean story about a metric rewarding the wrong thing.

It did something worse. It tied: same trustworthy count, same silently-wrong count, identical field accuracy and recall.

values a crew member can check, shipped 33
with the repair pass 0
cost +38%

Thirty-three derived values, each with its reasoning attached, become zero. The pass guessed right every time on this corpus, which is not a reason to keep it — the behaviour is identical whether the guess is right or wrong, so the only thing between a pilot and a confidently wrong report time is that the model happened to be correct.

hot take

A good metric is necessary, and it is not sufficient.

The two worst bugs in this project were in the measuring apparatus, not the thing being measured, and both would have flattered the result.

The grader compared timestamps as strings, so 08:00:00.000Z and 08:00:00Z counted as a misread — it reported 78% accuracy on a roster read perfectly. My answer key was wrong about days off, recording every one as being at base; the reader disagreed with me on six fields and was right about all six.

Then the fix for the last failing case turned out not to be a rule or a check, but asking the model to say which values it had worked out rather than read. Being made to show its work changed the work.

And the experiment I removed tied on every number I report while taking the count of checkable values from 33 to zero. My metric could not see the difference — which is worse than being fooled by it, because being fooled at least leaves a trace.

Build the scoreboard before the agent. Then distrust the scoreboard too, and go and look at what the person actually ends up holding.

Advisory only. Not a flight time limitations tool; it makes no ruling on whether any duty is legal. Every airline, roster and crew reference in the project is invented — no real person's schedule appears anywhere in it.
